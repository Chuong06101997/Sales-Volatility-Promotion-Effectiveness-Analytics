# Executive Summary

## The Problem

Two stores, 91 products, three years of daily sales — and demand planning still running on judgement. The result: stock-outs during peak periods, excess inventory during slow ones, and no clear answer to a basic question — is any of this predictable, or is the business reacting to noise?

## What the Data Showed

**Promotions work, and they don't appear to be borrowing from tomorrow's sales.** Promotion days sell 47% higher on average than a normal, season-adjusted day (12,702 promotion-days tested). In the three days after a promotion ends, sales return to normal — they don't dip below it. That second point matters commercially: a dip would mean the promotion just moved the timing of a sale that was going to happen anyway. No dip was found.

**Two "identical-looking" stores are barely different.** Sales volatility between the two stores is nearly the same (a 1% gap in one measure, a 4% gap in another). Whatever is driving performance differences at these locations, it isn't the store itself in any simple sense.

**Weekends and the June-to-August / November-December stretch are real, provable sales drivers** — worth roughly $80 and $30-40 extra per day respectively, even after accounting for promotions running at the same time. Weather was tested three separate ways and showed no measurable effect on sales — a useful negative result, since it rules out a factor planning teams sometimes assume matters.

**A simple, transparent forecast — no AI, no black box — gets within 28% of actual daily sales.** That's the baseline. It exists to answer one question before anyone builds something more complex: is a simple approach good enough to start with, or do we need to invest further? Right now, nobody has measured whether it beats what planners already do by instinct — because that comparison data was never collected.

## What We're Recommending

Three specific, low-risk moves — not a system purchase, not a reorganization:

1. **Direct planning attention to three specific product-store combinations** that show the highest combined volatility and stock-out risk.
2. **Pilot a modest increase in promotion frequency** on a limited set of products, watching profit margin closely — some of the deepest-discount promotions already run close to break-even.
3. **Run the simple forecast alongside current planning**, not instead of it, so the business finally has the comparison data to know if it's worth relying on.

## The Value

Sharper focus for a stretched planning team, a promotions strategy backed by evidence instead of habit, and the first real benchmark for judging whether future forecasting investment is worth it — without committing budget before that evidence exists.

## What We Can't Claim Yet

Two stores isn't enough data to say what drives performance *in general* — only what happened at these two. There's no cost data available, so we can't yet put a dollar figure on inventory savings or return on investment. And the promotion result is a strong pattern, not proof of cause and effect — no controlled experiment was possible with the data on hand. These aren't disclaimers added at the end; they shaped which recommendations made the cut and which didn't.

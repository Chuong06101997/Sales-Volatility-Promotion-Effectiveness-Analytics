# Executive Summary

## The Problem
Two stores, 91 products, three years of daily sales — and demand planning still running on judgement. The result: stock-outs during peak periods, excess inventory during slow ones, and no clear answer to a basic question — is any of this predictable, or is the business reacting to noise?

## What the Data Showed

**Promotions work, and they don't appear to be borrowing from tomorrow's sales.** Promotion days sell $151.53 higher on average than non-promotion days (12,702 promotion-days vs. 139,757 non-promotion-days tested). In the three days after a promotion ends, sales return to normal — they don't dip below it. That second point matters commercially: a dip would mean the promotion just moved the timing of a sale that was going to happen anyway. No dip was found.

**Two "identical-looking" stores are close in volatility, though the gap is statistically real.** Daily sales volatility is similar between the two stores (a coefficient of variation of 0.1097 vs. 0.1147), and a formal test confirms the two stores are not identical — but the practical size of that difference is small. Whatever is driving performance differences at these locations, it isn't the store itself in any large or simple way.

**Weekends and summer months are real, provable sales drivers** — weekends add roughly $79 extra per day on average, and summer is the strongest season for sales, running noticeably above the slowest season (spring). Weather was tested two separate ways and showed no measurable effect on sales — a useful negative result, since it rules out a factor planning teams sometimes assume matters.

**A simple, transparent forecast — no AI, no black box — gets within 28.63% of actual daily sales.** That's the baseline. It exists to answer one question before anyone builds something more complex: is a simple approach good enough to start with, or do we need to invest further? The one place it clearly struggles is promotion days, where error nearly doubles — a specific, fixable gap rather than a general failure. Right now, nobody has measured whether the baseline beats what planners already do by instinct — because that comparison data was never collected.

## What We're Recommending

Three specific, low-risk moves — not a system purchase, not a reorganization:

1. **Manually adjust forecasts on promotion days**, where the simple baseline's error is largest and most concentrated — this is the single most evidence-backed fix available before considering any bigger investment.
2. **Pilot a modest increase in promotion frequency** on a limited set of products, watching profit margin closely — margin has not yet been measured, so this pilot is also how that gap gets closed.
3. **Run the simple forecast alongside current planning**, not instead of it, so the business finally has the comparison data to know if it's worth relying on.

## The Value

Sharper focus for a stretched planning team, a promotions strategy backed by evidence instead of habit, and the first real benchmark for judging whether future forecasting investment is worth it — without committing budget before that evidence exists.

## What We Can't Claim Yet

Two stores isn't enough data to say what drives performance *in general* — only what happened at these two. There's no cost data available, so we can't yet put a dollar figure on inventory savings, return on investment, or promotion margin. And the promotion result is a strong pattern, not proof of cause and effect — no controlled experiment was possible with the data on hand. These aren't disclaimers added at the end; they shaped which recommendations made the cut and which didn't.
